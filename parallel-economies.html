Independent research paper · Fall 2025

Parallel Economies The Resilience of Sudan’s Informal Economic Systems During Humanitarian Intervention, 1983–1993

Chad Lickfelt · Florida International University

Read the full paper (PDF) Key findings
Summary

What the paper examines

This paper examines how hawala remittance networks, sanduk savings associations, and suq markets functioned as parallel economic infrastructure in Sudan during a decade of war, famine, and state contraction. It argues that these informal systems were not stopgaps but durable institutions, with direct implications for how humanitarian actors should engage local economies in protracted crises.

Hawala networks, Suq markets, and traditional financial systems did not collapse under the weight of humanitarian aid. Instead, they adapted, specialized, and continued offering practical services that formal systems could not replicate.
Findings

Four findings

  1. Most of the money moved outside official channels.

    Between 1970 and 1989, only 23 percent of Sudan’s remittances flowed through official channels (Elbadawi, 1994), and informal remittances averaged more than $1 billion a year through the 1980s (Brown, 1992). Government exchange-rate policy widened the gap between official and black-market rates, which pushed more people toward informal services.

  2. Three institutions did three different jobs.

    Hawala brokers moved money across borders on personal trust, without identification or paper contracts. Sanduks pooled savings and extended credit in villages that banks did not reach, and they were open to women. Suqs supplied goods, including through cross-border trade, and doubled as places where community networks were maintained and information was exchanged.

  3. Aid ran alongside these systems. It did not replace them.

    Many outsiders expected food distribution and formal finance to modernize and displace local institutions. That never materialized. Refugee camps and NGOs could operate only a limited number of sites, while suqs served a broader share of the population and offered a wider range of goods.

  4. Three factors explain the resilience.

    Functional specialization, superior service delivery grounded in local knowledge and trust, and distributive advantages in speed and reach. Underneath all three sits social capital: trust, mutual obligation, and flexible group adaptation.

Implications

What it means for humanitarian policy

The paper argues for integration over parallel delivery: humanitarian policy should build on existing informal systems instead of replacing them. In practice, that means contracting with hawala networks for aid distribution, partnering with suq markets for supply chain management, and enlisting sanduks to create community-level programming.

It also means measuring success differently: by the volume of aid moving through informal channels, the number of formal and informal partnerships created, and how these hybrid systems hold up in times of duress or governmental upheaval.

The parallel approach has failed Sudan repeatedly. Integration offers a more promising way forward.
